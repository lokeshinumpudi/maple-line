# The Things We Carried

Haru Morita, sixty, has driven the Maple Line for forty-one years. Yesterday he publicly endorsed its proposed winter timetable. He checked the single-track crossings, turnrounds, and crew breaks. He did not check the trips that continue after people leave his train.

Today his granddaughter Emi, seventeen, is making a follow-up recording for the village radio. The first problem is printed on the back of Haru’s draft: **Aonuma train arrival 17:42; last village bus 17:40**. As they work the service, Haru hears what his endorsement means for actual households. Emi discovers that recording people fairly is harder than collecting the most persuasive parts of what they say.

The campaign remains affectionate, sometimes funny, and interested in the view. Its conflict is present-day and specific. Haru’s competence matters; so does the work he has allowed other people to supply without asking.

## Source context and fictional boundaries

The [research note](research/JAPANESE-RAILWAY-LIFE.md) records ten findings from Japanese railway operators, municipal consultations and newsletters, and a national survey of older adults. These informed the story’s attention to connections, staff cover, school access, weather clearance, and individual working lives.

The characters, region, timetable, radio programme, bread contract, and trial proposal are fictional. The story does not reproduce a real family or suggest that one sixty-year-old represents Japanese grandparents. The particular railway’s operating rules still need specialist review before a release claims operational realism.

A road connection may be the right answer. A protected crew break may be non-negotiable. A passenger can support the railway and oppose a particular draft. Nobody has to become an enemy to create a difficult day.

## Dramatic question

Can Haru correct a promise he made publicly without making another promise on someone else’s behalf?

He likes being useful and trusted. A work request receives an immediate answer; family plans are often expected to fit around it. His first account of the timetable treats Keiko’s car as available and Emi’s future as something he can solve. He gradually learns to ask for the whole trip, the actual day, and the other person’s agreement.

Emi is not his infallible teacher. At the tunnel she realizes she cut Nao’s workable road alternative out of an interview, making the complaint more dramatic. She restores it and later asks Nao to approve the passage and how she is identified. Both protagonists have to stop making other people simpler for their own purposes.

Keiko’s objection is not merely that she is tired. She has booked a shared kiln firing and paid for her shelf. She had somewhere of her own to go. Her time enters the plan as a commitment, not as emergency cover.

## Cast

**Haru Morita, 60.** Working driver, husband, father, grandfather. His strengths are preparation, consistency, and calm during a hold. His fault is making personal availability sound unlimited, especially when somebody else bears the cost. No terminal diagnosis or mandatory retirement drives this story.

**Emi, 17.** Applying for a Saturday sound course in Harumi. She has already considered an alternative road route and staying with a friend. She wants an adult to ask what she has tried before solving the situation for her. She makes a real editorial mistake and corrects it.

**Keiko, 61.** Potter with booked work, colleagues, and an expectation that her family will ask before using her car and time. Her private call is not included in the radio programme without permission. She is not the household’s endless supply of patient wisdom.

**Haru’s adult son.** The missed concert remains part of their history, but the present question is what he needs now: help attending Emi’s open day, or an evening without another explanation of the roster. He does not instantly forgive everything to complete Haru’s arc.

**Nao.** Runs the Momiji bakery. A clinic consignment will miss its agreed handover. She can consider a later delivery or a shared road van, but needs an answer before renewing a contract. Her mother’s remembered generosity had labour behind it.

**Fumi.** Bicycle mechanic at Aonuma. She wants her long-borrowed spanner returned. She knows about Keiko’s firing but refuses to negotiate on Keiko’s behalf. Her workshop is a workplace with another bicycle waiting.

**Jun.** Grower sharing irrigation work with neighbours and family care with his sister. Different departures help different commitments. He does not want to be described as helpless.

**Mr. Endo.** Former hospital kitchen worker, now asking about his own outward and return connection. His medical details stay private; the transport problem is enough.

**Yuta.** Hinoki woodworker dispatching furniture parcels. His question about a missed consignment arrives while Haru is tempted to accept another shift. Practical work interrupts reflection.

**Mika.** Summit café operator. She needs an honest notice about inspection, update time, and any confirmed replacement service. Haru’s old rescue story does not establish what she can promise customers today.

## Choices and consequences

There are twenty-four authored choice options. Their immediate replies and journal entries differ. Fourteen short callback definitions reference earlier choices; only matching prior choices append their line to a later scene. These callbacks make the later account remember whether Haru asked about alternatives, admitted an assumption, retained his signed draft, or shared authorship.

The final programme has two distinct forms:

- **Public correction:** Haru speaks his mistaken endorsement aloud, then includes approved voices and the marked draft. His responsibility leads the piece.
- **Joint proposal:** Emi and Haru alternate question and operational constraint. Nao keeps her qualified alternative. Both authors are named; Haru submits a separate written correction rather than letting joint authorship dilute his responsibility.

Both endings leave an actual next step: a review of a proposed protected **17:50** connection on selected Saturdays, eight minutes after the **17:42** train. Operator approval remains pending. The railway crew’s break remains protected. The source never announces that the line is saved or the revised bus is already operating.

Choices are not a hidden kindness score. They change who speaks, what is acknowledged, and what document is carried forward. A joke or a practical question is not secretly a bad ending.

## Required actions and delivery modes

Aonuma declares the required `return-spanner` action: set the spanner on Fumi’s bench. Minato declares `amend-connection`: pin the corrected connection sheet, labelled as a proposal with approval pending. Dialogue before either action describes intention and preparation, not an already completed return or posting. The engine and world layer own task completion, persistence, and the visible prop change.

The bridge and tunnel use `delivery: 'rolling'`. Reading there must not command an artificial emergency stop in the middle of a bridge or mountain. Other beats retain `delivery: 'platform'` for host-controlled stopped conversations; the opening scenes still use the existing safe trackside staging rather than an invented platform.

The first two scenes set up the draft, the borrowed tool and Keiko’s availability before the original Momiji stop. Later intervals leave room for driving and observation. Current main-scene text is approximately ninety to one hundred and ten words before callbacks. Reading speed, interruptions, and full-route timing still require player testing.

## Narration direction

Give each speaker a task or object of attention: a bread label, a muddy boot, a folded private letter, grease on a hand, a clay-covered phone, a microphone level. Use those actions to interrupt and complicate what is said. Avoid closing every scene with a general lesson.

Haru is precise at work and less direct when embarrassed. Emi can be impatient and can edit too aggressively. Keiko’s short, literal answers carry more weight than a speech about sacrifice. Fumi does not abandon her next repair to become a counsellor. Nao wants a decision before her contract date.

Speaker labels identify the featured character; the voice score assigns first-person prose and each quoted turn separately. Emi narrates the tunnel, Akane and Harumi scenes, including their responses and recalled choices. Haru narrates the other scenes. Jun has a named voice identity. The authored score covers every quotation and retains resolved callback text. Generated narration still needs listening review and performance direction before it can stand in for an acted cast.

## Scope for a paid release

The product proposition is a short narrative train game in which driving duties and family choices belong to the same working day. Its commercial strength must come from the relationship, legible operational decisions, convincing people and places, and an ending the player helped shape.

The script revision is not evidence of a saleable finished game. Character models still need closer review against the approved art. Several named speakers need staged models, gestures and workplace props. The connection sheet, dispatch confirmations, consent decisions, roster changes and final programme need consistent visible feedback. Audio performances, access settings, tested save/resume, controller support, complete route pacing and target-hardware performance need release checks.

The first external playtest should ask whether a player understands the two-minute connection failure, why Haru backed the draft, why Keiko objects, and what actually changes by the ending. If the only remembered detail is the spanner, the revision has not done enough.

A $10,000 revenue target remains a commercial goal, not a prediction. A $15 list price would need 667 full-price sales to exceed $10,000 gross before fees, taxes, discounts and refunds. That arithmetic neither establishes demand nor recommends the price. The next production milestone is a coherent opening that strangers can play, followed by observed completion, feedback, and a realistic release budget.
